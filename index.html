18 mThe drawing

Drawn to the hundredth of a millimetre.

Before any steel is cut, every one of the watch's 212 parts exists as a line on a sheet like this one. We spend longer here than anywhere else. A tolerance you get wrong on paper is a gasket that weeps at 200 metres.

42 mThe case

Forty wide. Twelve point four deep.

Big enough to read with a glove on, thin enough to slide under a cuff. The lugs turn down to meet the wrist, so it sits flat instead of perched.

The crown screws down between two guards and only comes out when you mean it to.

104 mThe caseback

No window. On purpose.

Exhibition casebacks are lovely, and each one is another seal that can fail. The Sounding closes with solid steel on two gaskets, engraved with the depth chart of the Sognefjord, where every prototype is taken down.

160 mDisassembly

Nine parts that matter.

215 mThe bezel

It only turns one way.

Line the zero pip up with the minute hand as you go under. If the bezel gets knocked, it can only move toward less time remaining, never more. That is the whole point of it, and the reason we spent a year on one flat click spring.

300 mThe dial

At 300 metres there is no colour.

Red is gone by fifteen metres. The rest fades soon after, and below two hundred it is simply dark. What's left is whatever you painted on. BGW9 glows blue-white, the light your eyes hold onto longest.

300 mThe movement

Calibre K-21.

Built for us in the Jura to a short brief: run for three days, keep within six seconds a day, and survive a drop onto a boat deck. Take it off on Friday evening and it is still running on Monday morning.

375 mAssembly

Closed by hand. Tested to 375.

One watchmaker assembles each Sounding from start to finish. Then it spends an hour at 37.5 bar, a quarter beyond the rating on the dial, as ISO 6425 asks, before it is allowed to leave.

Materials and finishing

Brushed where you touch it. Polished where the light does.

A dive watch lives a rough life, so we finish it for its fifth year, not its first photograph. Surfaces that take knocks are brushed, which hides wear. Polish is kept to edges and chamfers, where it catches light and very little else.
